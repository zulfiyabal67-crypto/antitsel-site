I’m Over 40. Why Does Life Feel So Hard?

The emotional struggles we rarely talk about out loud

There is a certain age people talk about by saying, “The best is yet to come.”

But many women over 40 feel something quite different. Not inspiration. Not a surge of energy. Not a sense of new possibilities.

Instead, they feel tired and uncertain, with a question growing louder inside:

“Why is life so hard for me when everything looks fine from the outside?”

And from the outside, things may indeed look perfectly fine. Family. Work. Home. Children who are growing up or have already grown. Life goes on as usual.

Yet inside, there is a sense that something has stopped working—or perhaps has never worked the way you wanted it to.

This is not the midlife crisis of jokes and clichés. It can be something much deeper, and people rarely talk about it.

“I don’t know what I want”

This is one of the phrases women over 40 often say.

Not because they are indecisive or unable to make decisions.

But because, for years, they have known exactly what other people wanted: their children, partners, parents, managers, and society.

There was no time to want something of their own. Sometimes it felt inconvenient. Sometimes even shameful.

Then the children grow up. Or a career is established. Or, at last, a little free time appears.

And suddenly, there seems to be nothing inside.

Not necessarily depression. Not simply tiredness.

Emptiness.

When all the endless “I have to” thoughts fall away, there is nothing you can hear behind them.

This does not mean you are broken.

It is a signal.

You may simply never have been shown what to do with it.

“I’m tired of being strong”

Sometime after their thirties, many women begin to wear the identity of someone who can handle everything.

It helps them get through difficult times.

It earns respect.

It works.

But after 40, that identity can begin to feel like a burden.

Because behind it are years of having no permission to be vulnerable, to have doubts, or to say:

“I don’t know.”

“This is hard for me.”

“I can’t keep doing this.”

Sometimes, a woman says these words and frightens herself.

Being strong has become part of who she believes she is.

The strength that once served as a shield can gradually become a cage.

And then a particular kind of exhaustion arrives.

Not just from work.

Not from household chores.

But from the constant need to be the person who can withstand everything.

“I’m afraid the best years are behind me”

This fear is rarely spoken about openly.

But it can be there, quietly.

The fear that time has passed.

That important opportunities have been missed.

That it is too late to start something new.

Comparison feeds this fear.

Comparing yourself with women who have supposedly “made it.”

With the person you were at twenty-five.

With polished success stories on social media.

Gradually, the fear starts to sound like common sense.

But it is not necessarily realism.

It may be anxiety—the fear that, after living by other people’s expectations for so long, you will not have time to find your way back to yourself.

“I don’t know who I am without my roles”

Mother.

Wife.

Daughter.

Employee.

Friend.

We get so used to these roles that we can stop noticing the person behind them.

And one day, a question appears:

“Who am I if I set all of this aside?”

Many women over 40 ask themselves this question for the first time.

And the silence that follows can be frightening.

But that silence does not necessarily mean there is nothing inside you.

It may mean you have not spent much time close to yourself for a long while.

Children leaving home. A divorce. The end of a career. The beginning of a new stage of life.

Any of these transitions can bring a woman back to questions about who she is.

And that can hurt.

Because it is honest.

“No one really sees me”

Loneliness after 40 does not always look like being alone.

There may be people around you.

Family.

Colleagues.

Friends.

And still, you may feel that no one knows who you really are.

Because you have long been used to being accommodating.

Easy to understand.

Predictable.

Someone who does not cause problems.

Someone who does not burden others with difficult feelings.

Over time, an entire inner world can build up that no one else knows about.

The silence becomes harder to carry.

And one day, you may long for someone to finally see the real you.

Without roles.

Without obligations.

Without the need to be strong.

What can you do with all of this?

The first step is not to fix yourself.

Not to force yourself to feel motivated.

Not to make yourself “productive” again at any cost.

The first step is to acknowledge that this is really happening.

That things feel hard not because you are weak, but perhaps because you have been carrying a great deal for a very long time.

That inner emptiness is not, by itself, a diagnosis.

It may be an invitation to get to know yourself again.

A woman over 40 has not missed her chance to live.

She has not necessarily missed her own life.

Perhaps this is the moment when a first real opportunity to choose herself begins to appear.

The Antigoal Method

The Antigoal method is not about setting new goals, achieving more, or endlessly improving yourself.

It begins somewhere else.

With making room by noticing what no longer belongs:

  • other people’s expectations;
  • “shoulds” imposed from outside;
  • inner pressure;
  • the habit of living against yourself.

Only then can you look at what remains.

Sometimes that feels frightening.

But often, it can also bring a sense of relief.
